Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  1. Explain why observers in relative motion disagree about time intervals and lengths.
  2. Identify proper time and proper length in a given situation.
  3. Calculate the Lorentz factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) and use it to find dilated times and contracted lengths.
  4. Resolve the twin paradox by identifying what makes the two twins' situations different.

Time dilation

LESSON 1

Predict

A light clock ticks once per second for an astronaut in a moving rocket. Will someone on the ground count more, fewer, or the same number of seconds for one of those ticks? Why?

Open the simulation →

Explore

  1. Set the speed to 0.6c. Compare the path of the light pulse in the ground frame and in the rocket frame. Which path is longer?
  2. Record γ at 0.3c, 0.6c, 0.8c and 0.95c. Describe how γ behaves as v approaches c.
  3. Follow the section on where the formula comes from, and write the derivation in your own words.

Check your understanding

1. At v = 0.6c, how much ground time passes for one 1-second tick of the rocket clock?
γ = 1/√(1 − 0.36) = 1/0.8 = 1.25, so Δt = γΔt₀ = 1.25 s.
2. In this setup, which observer measures the proper time? Why?
The astronaut. Proper time is measured by a clock at rest relative to both events, and both the emission and return of the light pulse happen at the same place in the rocket.
3. Muons have a lifetime of about 2.2 μs at rest. At v = 0.995c, how long do they last as seen from Earth?
γ = 1/√(1 − 0.995²) ≈ 10.0, so about 22 μs. This is why muons made in the upper atmosphere survive long enough to reach the ground.

Length contraction

LESSON 2

Predict

If moving clocks run slow, what do you think happens to the length of a moving rocket as measured from the ground? Does its height change too?

Open the simulation →

Explore

  1. Increase the speed step by step and watch the rocket in the ground frame. Which dimension changes, and which stays the same?
  2. Switch to the rocket frame. What does the astronaut measure for the rocket's own length?
  3. Use the graph to find the speed at which the rocket appears half its rest length.

Check your understanding

1. A rocket is 40 m long at rest. How long is it measured from the ground at 0.8c? Check your answer with the simulation.
γ = 1/√(1 − 0.64) = 1/0.6 ≈ 1.667, so L = L₀/γ = 40 × 0.6 = 24 m.
2. Who measures the proper length of the rocket?
The astronaut, or anyone at rest relative to the rocket. Proper length is always the longest length any observer measures.
3. At what speed is the rocket measured at half its rest length?
When γ = 2: 1 − v²/c² = 1/4, so v = (√3/2)c ≈ 0.866c.

The twin paradox

LESSON 3

Predict

One twin travels to a distant star and back at high speed. Each twin sees the other's clock running slow during the trip. So who is younger at the reunion, or are they the same age?

Open the simulation →

Explore

  1. Run the journey and watch both clocks. Note each twin's age at the reunion.
  2. Study the spacetime map. Which twin's worldline is straight, and which one bends? What does the bend represent physically?
  3. Read what each twin actually sees during the trip, and note when each twin sees the other's flashes switch from arriving slowly to arriving quickly.

Check your understanding

1. A star is 4 light-years away. The traveller goes there and back at 0.8c. How much does each twin age? Check your answer with the simulation.
Earth twin: 8 ly ÷ 0.8c = 10 years. Traveller: 10 ÷ γ = 10 × 0.6 = 6 years. The traveller is 4 years younger.
2. Why isn't the situation symmetric, even though each twin sees the other moving?
Only the travelling twin changes inertial frames at the turnaround (and feels the acceleration). The Earth twin stays in one inertial frame the whole time, so the two worldlines are not equivalent.

Wrap-up reflection

In a short paragraph, explain to a Grade 12 student why "time is relative" does not mean "anything goes." Mention proper time, the Lorentz factor, and what all observers do agree on (the speed of light).
