The twin paradox

One twin flies to a star and comes back. Each twin sends a flash of light on every birthday, so at the reunion neither of them can argue with the count.

What each twin actually sees

The split is the whole resolution.

It was never symmetric

Only one twin turns around. The rocket twin fires engines, changes direction, and switches from one inertial frame to another. The Earth twin never does.

So the usual objection — "each sees the other as the moving one, so who ages less?" — rests on a symmetry that is not there.

Misconception alert

The acceleration does not do the ageing. Make the turnaround as brief and as gentle as you like and the age gap barely changes; what matters is that the rocket's route through spacetime is the shorter one.

And the travelling twin never feels time drag. Six years aboard feel like six years.

For your future classroom

Have the class count flashes instead of arguing. Each twin sends one per birthday, so the number received equals the other twin's total ageing. Counting cannot depend on your frame, and the argument ends there.

The effect is measured, not hypothetical: Hafele and Keating flew atomic clocks around the world in 1971, and particle accelerators rely on it daily.

The trip is compressed to about nine seconds whatever settings you choose, so the flash pattern stays watchable.
